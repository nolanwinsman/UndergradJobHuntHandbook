# Tips for Once You've Actually Started the Job

## Be Useful

The biggest piece of advice I can give to any new hire for a new grad position or internship, is to find a way to be useful.

## Document Document Document

Documentation is so much more than just code comments. There are so many different things and ways you should be documenting, here are just some examples. Documenting is also a fantastic way to stand out as most people do the bare minimum. I want you to start thinking
of documentation like how board games are documented. They have instruction books, lists of all the pieces, examples, an overview of what the game is, ect. You should document your code in a similar manner.

Documentation isn't just for code also. I am slowly working my way through documenting every task for my job as I want whatever new grad is hired in the future to have an easier time than I did.

Below are just some examples
of documentation that you should consider doing.

### Documenting Your Repos

- What is this code
- How to install it
- How to run it
- Overview of every relevant file
- Who contributed to this code

## Precedent

Here's a goofy example. Let's say your neighbor is going on vacation for two weeks. He asks you to water his plants while you're gone. After two weeks he's back in town. He says hi, you ask about his vacation, ya'll talk for a bit. Ten days later he comes to your house
slamming his fist at your door screaming at you. You open the door and he's yelling profanities and screaming about his dying plants and how you let them die. "MY PLANTS HAVEN'T BEEN WATERED IN TEN DAYS!" You realize he expected you to water his plants even when he came
back from vacation. This makes no sense and you protest this but the HOA gets involved and they see security footage of you watering his plants and they decide you need to keep watering your plants. This is all delusional to you and makes no sense but you're still stuck
watering this guy's plants till the end of time.

Now this is certainly an exageration, but I still want ya'll to be cautious of things like this happening in the corporate world. There is some BS work my team is stuck with because ten years ago our team took on some work as a temporary favor and now we're still doing
this work since we set a precedent of doing said work. I wasn't there ten years ago. Why do I have to do this work? It's really stupid but there are quirks like this in the corporate world. To avoid things like this you need strong evidence which the next section should
cover.

Another example of precedent is what you're willing to do. When I first started my current job, I was paid overtime for any hours over 40 in a week. Now my boss is fantastic but her boss told her to not let Nolan work over 40 hours a week. I think that's reasonable
but a decent amount of my team's work is outside the 9:00 to 5:00. Sometimes we have 4AM installs, Saturday installs, I had a lot of late night meetings with our overseas folks. Whenever I would have to fill in these extra hours outside of the normal working hours
my boss would just ask me to clock out early on Fridays. I absolutely hated this. First of all Friday afternoons were pretty calm anyway so I wasn't avoiding that much work. And two, I missed out on a LOT of money doing this. I agreed to do it once but that somehow
became my precedent. I probably missed out on a few thousand dollars by doing this. After six months I went to my boss and I made it very clear that I am not doing this anymore. She agreed and I got my overtime hours. I made a LOT of money doing this. I remember
there was one time I had to work like six hours on MLK day and I made about $800 after tax for working on a holiday.

I hope all of this explains the importance of setting good precedents and not setting bad ones.

## Use Evidence to Protect Yourself

I hope you read the previous section on **Precedent**. Let's say a team asked you to cover some of their work temporarily. Normally I would shut them down and say no but maybe you need a favor from this team so you want to help them out. I would have STRONG email
evidence stating EXACTLY how long you will be taking over this task and making sure the email is very clear on them resuming ownership of said task at a specific date. The more evidence the better. You shouldn't use Slack/Teams messages or verbal confirmations, emails
are usually best.

The only thing better than email evidence is ticket evidence. At most big corporate jobs, everything is a ticket/request. If you need a new computer, you open a ticket for it. If you want PTO, you open a request. If you want a team to do something for you, you usually
open a ticket for it. The spark notes for this is if someone from another team asks you to do something, you should have a ticket that they opened with the EXACT parameters for their request.

Let's say a team wants you to delete a file on a server. They should open a ticket for you that has at least this information

- Exact name of server.
- Absolute File path.

And probably more things. But with this let's say a week later someone comes to you freaking out about why their file was deleted, you have a ticket showing proof that you didn't just do that for fun.
